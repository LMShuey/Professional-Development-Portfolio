## What I Actually Did — And Why It Transfers

Most fraud detection work — excellent, high-skill work — operates within 
established frameworks: known indicators, proven methods, documented playbooks. 
That foundation exists for good reason and it works.

What my unit did was different. We were building the framework for new 
investigative concepts ourselves.

As a Forensic Investigator in the Special Operations unit of a major national 
insurer, I was part of a small team actively building methodology from concept 
to usage — no manual, no precedent, no established workflow to follow. This 
unit was created by direct CEO order specifically to address a gap that earlier 
casework had exposed — and we were expected to fill it. Our law enforcement 
partners and agency collaborators (state AGs, U.S. Attorneys, CA BAR) and 
industry partners (NICB) brought us problems that often required novel 
approaches which we delivered. We designed the operation, stress-tested it 
against expert adversaries, built the evidence framework, and translated highly 
technical physical findings for lay audiences including juries.

In current AI terminology, that work involved:

- **Adversarial operational design** — engineering undercover operations 
  specifically to defeat detection by domain-expert targets
- **Anomaly detection methodology** — building layered, multi-signal 
  statistical frameworks to surface fraud patterns across large claim 
  populations while controlling for legitimate variables
- **Bias-resistant evaluation** — maintaining outcome-neutral investigative 
  standards throughout; the goal was always what the evidence actually showed, 
  including fully clearing innocent parties when evidence supported it — 
  a result treated as equally valid as a fraud finding
- **Cross-audience translation** — documenting complex physical evidence 
  (forensic vehicle inspection, covert UV markings, microdot tracking) in ways 
  that were credible and readily understandable to non-expert juries and 
  legal teams
- **Novel tool development** — when existing tools didn't exist, we built them; 
  when existing technology didn't reach far enough, we sourced better technology

It came from a direct CEO mandate, law enforcement partners' needs and 
requests, and the expectation that we would solve problems no existing playbook 
addressed.

That's the transferable skill. The domain was auto and property repair fraud. 
The cognitive work was exactly what AI training and evaluation teams need: 
building evaluation frameworks under adversarial conditions, adapting 
methodology in real time, and maintaining calibrated judgment without 
institutional scaffolding.
